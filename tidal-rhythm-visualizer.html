Tidal Rhythm

How the Moon and Sun raise — and time — Earth's tides.

The Moon doesn't just tug water toward itself — it stretches the whole Earth into an oval. One bulge faces the Moon. A second, equal bulge forms on the far side, where Earth's pull on the water is weakest. Press play, or drag the slider, to spin Earth through one full tidal day.

Gravity follows the inverse square law (1/r²) — by that law alone, the Sun's gravity on Earth is about 180× stronger than the Moon's. But tides aren't caused by gravity's strength, they're caused by how much it differs across Earth's diameter. That difference falls off as 1/r³ — an inverse cube law. Cube the Sun's much greater distance, and its tidal advantage evaporates: the Moon, despite being 27 million times less massive, wins by more than 2 to 1. See the inverse square law →

Once a month, the Sun and Moon line up — and their pulls add together. Twice a month they sit at right angles, and partly cancel. Press play to watch the Moon revolve through a full month and see the combined bulge stretch and round out in real time.

In the idealized equilibrium-tide model, spring range ≈ (1 + 1/2.2) of the Moon-alone range, and neap range ≈ (1 − 1/2.2) of it — a predicted spring-to-neap ratio of about 2.7×. Real coastlines rarely match this exactly (more on that in Real Tide Data) — but the monthly rhythm itself is unmistakable.

Same forcing, different coastline

Around the same May–June 2026 new moon, Kings Point's spring high tide reached 9.2 ft — while Montauk Point's, facing the open Atlantic just 70 miles east, reached only 3.6 ft. Long Island Sound narrows like a funnel toward its western end, and the incoming tidal wave resonates and piles up against that geometry. The Moon and Sun set the rhythm everywhere identically — the coastline sets the size.
