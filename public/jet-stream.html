The Jet Stream

Where cold polar air meets warm subtropical air, the temperature contrast itself becomes a river of fast wind.

Why it exists: the jet stream is a narrow ribbon of fast wind, 9–12 km up, that forms where cold polar air meets warmer air from the south. Temperature contrast is the engine — the sharper the contrast, the faster the core wind. Earth's rotation then deflects that flow eastward (the Coriolis effect), which is why the jet — and the broader westerly flow around it — moves predominantly west to east across North America. Watch the core closely: you'll see brighter surges slide along it. Those are jet streaks — real, localized speed maxima that travel along the jet and help trigger storms at the surface near their leading and trailing edges.
