Shaded band = |β| below 1.58°, the ecliptic limit. Cross the ecliptic (β = 0°) at a new or full moon and you are at a node — that is the only way to land inside it.

Every dot is computed live, not looked up: syzygy times and γ come from Meeus's lunation series (Astronomical Algorithms, ch. 49 & 54), and the eclipse type comes from the same shadow-cone geometry used in steps 1–3. Checked against the published 2026–2056 catalogue: all 140 real eclipses found on the right day, one extra grazing penumbral flagged, γ accurate to 0.002 Earth radii, and 139 of 140 types reproduced.

The Saros — why the same eclipse comes back

Three lunar clocks nearly agree after 6,585⅓ days: 223 synodic months, 242 draconic months, 239 anomalistic months. Same phase, same node, same distance — so the eclipse repeats, 18 years 11⅓ days later, shifted 120° west. Click any dot to trace its family.

The three months that nearly agree
The three bars end within 5 hours of one another after 18 years. That near-miss is the whole Saros: come back one Saros later and the Sun, the node and the Moon's distance are all almost exactly where they were.
