Why twice per orbit?
The Moon's orbit (gold loop) is tilted 5.15° to the ecliptic — Earth's orbital plane, shown here as the flat teal ring. It crosses that plane at two nodes every 27.2 days. The white marker is the Moon's current position; the dotted stem shows how far above or below the ecliptic it is right now. An eclipse needs a new/full Moon to land near one of the two crossings — not just anywhere on the tilted loop.

β oscillates through a full swing roughly every 173 days — an eclipse season. That beat, between the 29.53-day synodic month and the 27.21-day draconic month, is the real reason eclipses cluster twice a year instead of happening every month.

A Saros is 18 years, 11⅓ days — 223 synodic months ≈ 239 anomalistic months ≈ 242 draconic months. Sun, Moon and node realign almost exactly, so the eclipse repeats with nearly the same character, shifted about 120° west in longitude (the extra ⅓ day) and drifting slowly toward one pole. Every third Saros (~54 years) lands near the same longitudes again — an exeligmos.
